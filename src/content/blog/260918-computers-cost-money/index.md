As of recently, I have been in search to rent a server computer.

Like many other things, there are forums dedicated to the hobbyist usage of rental computers, an accumulation of some two decades of operating within The Internet.
Renting a server for about $5/month has been a decent deal for most people, but what's surprising to me is how *low* you can actually go with costs.
The webpage I'm writing on right now is pure text, and I host it free online, funded by the generous spending of corporations on these web companies.
The server I went with costs *$20 a year*, which is absurdly low for a computer being online 24/7. I'd imagine the electric bill already costs half of that.

Such low costs only comes with very small and lean operations (for example, from what I know my server provider company is a single person).
They also depend a lot on much older hardware (think Intel Xeons and DDR4 RAM), and offer much lower guarantee on reliability. For personal purposes, that is fine.

Another topic I see around these vendors is how their prices have increased in recent months.
Smaller businesses struggle more as hardware costs increased, with a smaller potential customer pool.
Furthermore, such pool would be low on enterprise clients, as they would be requiring customer supports and contractual obligations, ultimately reducing to personal use.
As much as I want to avoid mentioning AI on here, it definitely is relevant -- the costs of personal computing has gone up, as priorities of hardware producers shift.

## How much does it cost to be online?
For a budget of $5 a month, I'm certainly going shopping for deals.
It's reasonable for the use case and benefits, but it is not *free*.
Does make me think about the many things online that I use.

One reason a service is offered free or low-cost is to increase adoption. It sounds like Business 101.
Social media's main appeal grows the more users sign up, so they are free. In turn, they sell ads.
Advertisements has been the method for free websites since long, but the profits of these companies are in the billions of dollars, which is a whole different *scale*:
Targeted advertising and dark patterns are prevalent, the use of cookies to track your activities across websites, all work together to squeeze out advertising revenue on you and I.
We pay through spending more time and attention than needed on these platforms, and that is probably the most costly thing.

## The Internet is still a marvel.
I don't think I can put a value to my access to YouTube.
I pay for YouTube Premium (it's cheaper with family), and I think having the world's knowledge and entertainment without ads is a privilege very much worth the money.

However, now that the Internet has coalesced around a few mega companies, *enshittification* ensued.
*Both paying and free users* get worse treatment, and I think people are feeling it.

Running and hosting things on computers cost money, but it has been so low to the point of negligible, for the benefit such operation brings.
The cost/value balance has shifted deeply - squeezing revenue makes using Internet services increasingly more hostile.
It's like a kind of negative externality, a pollution of public cognition.

I'll camp around the forums, but it's not such a no-brainer as before. Maybe when I make more money.
